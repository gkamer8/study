### Key terms

- **Benchmark**: a fixed dataset plus metric used to compare methods.
- **Zero-shot**: making predictions for a task without training on any of its labels.
- **Fine-tuning**: further training a pretrained model on task labels.
- **Probing**: training a small model on top of a frozen model's embeddings.
- **Embedding**: a model's internal vector representation of an input, such as a protein or a cell.
- **Masked language model (MLM)**: a model trained to fill in hidden tokens (ESM-2, DNABERT).
- **Autoregressive model**: a model that predicts each next token from the previous ones (ProGen, Evo).
- **Log-likelihood**: the log probability a model assigns to a sequence or token.
- **Token**: the unit a model reads.
- **Tokenization**: how raw data is split into the units a model reads.
- **k-mer**: a substring of length k, e.g. 6 bases.
- **One-hot encoding**: representing each base as a vector with a single 1 (A = 1000, C = 0100…).
- **Context length**: how much sequence a model sees at once.
- **Fitness**: how well a protein variant performs a measured function.
- **DMS**: measuring the fitness of thousands of variants of one protein in a single experiment.
- **Variant effect prediction (VEP)**: predicting whether a sequence change is harmful.
- **Sequence identity**: the percentage of aligned positions where two sequences share the same residue.
- **Homolog**: a sequence related to another by common ancestry (see Unit 4).
- **Data leakage**: test examples too similar to training examples, which inflates scores.
- **Held-out**: deliberately kept out of training for testing.
- **Foundation model**: a large model pretrained on broad data and adapted to many tasks.
- **Perturbation**: an experimental change to cells (gene knockout, drug) whose effect is measured.
- **Spearman's ρ**: correlation between two rankings, from −1 to 1.
- **Contact**: two residues close together in 3D, typically under 8 Å apart.

## What problem is this paper solving?

ML-for-biology papers fall into a handful of task families. Identify which one, then ask: what is the input, what is the label, where did the data come from, and how was it split into train and test?

- **Structure prediction**: amino acid sequence → 3D coordinates (AlphaFold2/3, ESMFold, RoseTTAFold). The key **benchmark** is **CASP** (Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction), a blind competition held every two years since 1994. Predictors submit models for structures that are solved but not yet public, and the models are scored with GDT_TS and lDDT (see Unit 4). CAMEO runs continuously. AlphaFold2 dominated CASP14 (2020).
- **Variant effect prediction (VEP)**: given a mutation, predict whether it damages the protein or causes disease. Models: EVE, ESM-1v, AlphaMissense, GEMME, and SpliceAI for splicing. Labels come from **DMS** experiments or ClinVar (see Unit 3).
- **Fitness prediction and protein design**: score or generate sequences with a desired property (stability, binding, activity). **Inverse folding** (ProteinMPNN) designs a sequence that should fold into a given backbone shape. RFdiffusion generates new backbones.
- **Regulatory genomics**: DNA sequence → measured regulatory signals along the genome (expression, accessibility, TF binding; see Unit 6). Models: Basenji, Enformer, Borzoi, and DNA language models (DNABERT, Nucleotide Transformer, HyenaDNA, Caduceus, Evo).
- **Single-cell foundation models**: pretrain on tens of millions of single-cell expression profiles (Geneformer, scGPT, scFoundation, UCE), then **fine-tune** (keep training on task labels) or **probe** (fit a small model on frozen **embeddings**, the model's internal vectors). Tasks: cell-type annotation, batch integration, predicting responses to **perturbations** (knockouts or drugs), and gene-network inference.
- **Function annotation**: predict Gene Ontology terms, enzyme classes, or interactions (the CAFA benchmark).

## Deep mutational scanning and ProteinGym

**Fitness** here means how well a protein variant performs some measured function. **Deep mutational scanning (DMS)** measures it at scale. Researchers build a library containing nearly every possible single amino acid change in a protein (**saturation mutagenesis**), put cells carrying the library under selection (growth, binding, fluorescence, stability), and sequence before and after. Variants that become more common scored well; variants that disappear scored badly. One experiment yields fitness scores for thousands of variants.

**ProteinGym** (Notin et al., 2023) is the standard benchmark built from such data: **217 DMS substitution assays (~2.5M variants)**, 66 assays of insertions/deletions, and ClinVar clinical variant sets. Models are mostly tested **zero-shot**, without seeing any fitness labels: does the model's score rank variants in the same order as the experiment? The headline metric is **Spearman's ρ**, the correlation between two *rankings* (1 = identical order, 0 = unrelated). It is computed per assay and averaged across assays, grouped by function type (activity, binding, expression, organismal fitness, stability). Secondary metrics: AUC, MCC, NDCG, top-k recall. Strong models average around 0.4–0.5, so the task is far from solved. Spearman is used because each assay measures something different on its own scale, often nonlinear, so only the order of scores is comparable.

**Zero-shot scoring with a protein language model.** A **masked language model** (ESM-2) is trained to guess hidden amino acids from their context. To score a mutation, hide that position and compare the model's probabilities. The **masked marginal score** = log p(mutant amino acid) − log p(wild-type amino acid), where wild-type is the original. A negative score means the model finds the mutant less plausible. **Autoregressive** models (which predict the next token, like GPT) instead compare the **log-likelihood** of the whole mutant and wild-type sequences. The assumption underneath both: what evolution keeps (plausible sequences) ≈ what functions (fitness).

## What gets tokenized

A **token** is the unit the model reads, and **tokenization** is how raw data gets split into those units.

- **Proteins**: almost always **one token per amino acid** (~20 letters plus special tokens) in ESM-2, ProGen and ESM3. ESM3 adds tokens for structure and function. Typical lengths are a few hundred to ~1,000 residues, and longer proteins are cropped.
- **DNA**: the choice matters.
  - **Single nucleotide**: HyenaDNA, Caduceus, Evo, and Enformer/Borzoi, which use **one-hot** input where each base becomes a 4-dimensional vector with a single 1.
  - **Overlapping k-mers**: DNABERT uses substrings of length 3–6 that overlap. Because neighboring tokens share bases, a masked token can be read off its neighbors.
  - **Non-overlapping 6-mers**: Nucleotide Transformer.
  - **BPE** (byte-pair encoding, as in LLMs): DNABERT-2.
  Other issues: **context length**, since enhancers act ~100 kb away (see Unit 1); **reverse-complement equivariance**, since a sequence and its reverse complement (the other strand; see Unit 1) carry the same information and should get consistent predictions, which Caduceus builds in; and the fact that much of the genome is repetitive or non-functional.
- **Genes as tokens (single-cell)**: a cell is represented as a set of gene tokens. **Geneformer** uses **rank-value encoding**: genes are sorted by expression relative to their typical level across the corpus, and the values themselves are dropped. **scGPT** pairs gene tokens with **binned expression values**. There is no natural order, most values are zero, and batch effects (see Unit 6) are large. Several studies found simple baselines (highly variable genes + PCA, linear models, or predicting the mean training perturbation) match or beat foundation models on some tasks, so read single-cell foundation model claims critically.

For single cells there is no natural order: Geneformer creates one by ranking genes, and scGPT instead attaches a binned value to each gene. Examples are illustrative.

## Data splits and leakage

**Data leakage** means test examples are too similar to training examples, so scores overstate how well the model generalizes. Biology data are full of near-duplicates: proteins have **homologs** (see Unit 4), genomes have repeats and gene copies, and cells share donors and batches. **Random splits overestimate performance.**

- **Sequence identity clustering**: **sequence identity** is the percent of aligned positions with the same residue. Cluster all sequences with **MMseqs2** (`easy-cluster --min-seq-id 0.3`) or **CD-HIT** (whose protein mode only goes down to ~40%), then put whole clusters in train or test. Common thresholds: 30%, 40%, 50%, 90%. Lower is stricter and more honest.
- **Fold/superfamily splits** (CATH/SCOP; see Unit 4) for structure tasks, because related proteins can share a fold below 30% identity.
- **Temporal splits**: train on structures released before a date and test on later ones (CASP-style).
- **Pretraining contamination**: a model pretrained on UniRef has seen the original sequences of the DMS proteins. That is usually accepted for zero-shot benchmarks, but it matters for novelty claims.
- **Genomics**: hold out **entire chromosomes** (e.g. chr8 and chr9 as test). Gene copies on other chromosomes can still leak.
- **Variant-effect circularity**: ClinVar labels were partly assigned using computational predictors, and well-studied genes dominate. If variants from the same genes appear in both train and test, a model can learn "this gene is usually pathogenic" instead of anything about the variant (Grimm et al. 2015). Hold out whole genes.
- **DMS**: random splits within one protein let a model learn "position 50 is sensitive" from neighbors at the same position. Use position-based (modulo) or contiguous-region splits.
- **Single-cell**: split by donor, study or batch, not by cell. For perturbation prediction, hold out whole perturbations or cell types.
- **Protein interactions and drug–target pairs**: hold out proteins, not just pairs, or the model memorizes that one protein binds everything.

## Metrics glossary

- **Spearman ρ**: rank correlation. Standard for DMS and fitness.
- **AUROC / AUPRC**: classification metrics (pathogenic vs benign). AUROC is the area under the true-positive vs false-positive curve. Prefer **AUPRC** (area under the precision–recall curve) when positives are rare.
- **Pearson r**: linear correlation, often on log-counts in genomics track prediction.
- **lDDT, GDT_TS, TM-score, RMSD**: structure accuracy (see Unit 4). DockQ for interfaces.
- **Precision@L**: for **contact** prediction (residue pairs within ~8 Å), the fraction of the top L predicted contacts that are real, where L = sequence length. Long-range contacts (|i−j| ≥ 24 positions apart) are the hard ones.
- **Perplexity**: exp(average negative log-likelihood). Lower means the model fits the data better, but it is only loosely tied to downstream usefulness.

## How to read one of these papers fast

1. Task and labels: DMS, ClinVar, structures, genomic tracks, or cell types?
2. Pretraining data and size (UniRef50 vs UniRef90, CELLxGENE, how many species' genomes).
3. Tokenization and context length.
4. Split strategy: does it control for homology, chromosome, donor or gene?
5. Baselines: is there a strong simple one (BLOSUM, conservation, mean prediction, PCA)?
6. Metric and aggregation: per-assay average or everything pooled?

## How you'll see this in a paper

- "Average Spearman across 217 ProteinGym DMS assays": zero-shot fitness benchmark.
- "Clustered at 30% sequence identity with MMseqs2 to prevent leakage."
- "Evaluated on held-out chromosomes 8 and 9."
- "Rank-value encoding of the top 2,048 genes": Geneformer-style input.
- "ipTM > 0.8 used to filter designed binders": structure confidence used as a design filter.
