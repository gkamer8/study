### Key terms

- **Amino acid**: a protein building block: a shared backbone part plus a distinctive side chain.
- **Side chain (R group)**: the variable part of an amino acid that gives it its chemistry.
- **Peptide bond**: the link joining one amino acid's carboxyl group to the next one's amino group.
- **Residue**: one amino acid within a protein chain, e.g. "position 175".
- **Backbone**: the repeating chain of atoms formed by peptide bonds, excluding side chains.
- **N-terminus**: the start of a protein chain, with a free amino group.
- **C-terminus**: the end of a protein chain, with a free carboxyl group.
- **Polypeptide**: an unbranched chain of amino acids.
- **Hydrophobic**: "water-fearing"; describes oily side chains buried inside proteins and membranes.
- **Polar**: "water-loving"; describes side chains that interact with water.
- **Charged**: carrying a net positive or negative charge at the cell's near-neutral pH.
- **Hydrogen bond**: a weak attraction between an H atom on N or O and another N or O; holds helices and sheets together.
- **Fold**: the overall 3D arrangement of a protein chain.
- **Domain**: a compact, independently folding unit of a protein.
- **Homolog**: a protein or gene related to another by common ancestry.
- **MSA**: many related sequences lined up position by position, so each column is an equivalent position.
- **Ångström (Å)**: 10⁻¹⁰ m; bonded atoms are ~1–2 of these apart.
- **Enzyme**: a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction.
- **Substrate**: the molecule an enzyme acts on.
- **Active site**: the pocket where an enzyme's reaction happens.
- **Ligand**: any molecule that binds a protein, such as a hormone or drug.
- **Receptor**: a protein that binds a signal and relays it, often spanning the membrane.
- **Transmembrane (TM)**: crossing the cell membrane.
- **ATP**: the cell's energy currency and the phosphate donor for kinases.
- **Antibody**: an immune protein that binds one specific target molecule.
- **Antigen**: the target molecule an antibody binds.
- **Inhibitor**: a molecule (often a drug) that blocks a protein's activity.

## Reading a protein sequence

A protein is a chain of **amino acids** linked by **peptide bonds**. Every amino acid shares the same backbone atoms (an amino group, a central carbon, a carboxyl group) and differs by its **side chain**. The chain has a start, the **N-terminus** (free amino group), and an end, the **C-terminus** (free carboxyl group). Sequences are always written N→C. Each amino acid in a chain is called a **residue**, so "R175" means the residue at position 175 is arginine. Proteins nearly always begin with M (methionine, from the start codon; see Unit 1).

There are 20 standard amino acids, written with **one-letter codes**. Learn them by chemistry. **Hydrophobic** ("water-fearing", oily) side chains cluster inside proteins and in membrane-crossing segments. **Polar** or **charged** side chains face the surrounding water and form bonds.

| Class | Residues | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrophobic | **A** Ala, **V** Val, **L** Leu, **I** Ile, **M** Met | buried core, transmembrane helices |
| Aromatic (ring-containing) | **F** Phe, **W** Trp, **Y** Tyr | W is the largest; Y can be phosphorylated |
| Polar, uncharged | **S** Ser, **T** Thr, **N** Asn, **Q** Gln | S/T phosphorylated; N glycosylated |
| Positively charged (basic) | **K** Lys, **R** Arg, **H** His | H is ~neutral at pH 7 and common in active sites and metal binding |
| Negatively charged (acidic) | **D** Asp, **E** Glu | |
| Special | **G** Gly, **P** Pro, **C** Cys | G: tiny and flexible. P: rigid ring, breaks helices. C: forms disulfide bonds |

The letters to drill are the ones that don't match the name: **F** Phe, **Y** Tyr, **W** Trp, **K** Lys, **R** Arg, **D** Asp, **E** Glu, **N** Asn, **Q** Gln. In sequence files: **X** = unknown, **B** = D or N, **Z** = E or Q, **U** = selenocysteine and **O** = pyrrolysine (rare extra amino acids), `*` = stop, `-` = gap in an alignment.

This chemistry predicts variant effects. Swapping similar residues (I→V) is usually mild. Reversing a charge (E→K), putting proline into a helix, replacing a glycine in a tight turn, or exposing a buried hydrophobic residue is usually damaging. **Substitution matrices** like **BLOSUM62** score how often each swap occurs between related proteins in nature, and they are used in alignment and as a variant-effect baseline.

## Levels of structure

- **Primary**: the sequence.
- **Secondary**: local shapes held by **hydrogen bonds** (weak attractions between backbone N–H and C=O groups). In the **α-helix**, a spiral with 3.6 residues per turn, residue i bonds to residue i+4. **β-strands** are extended segments that pair side by side into **β-sheets**: *parallel* if the neighboring strands run the same N→C direction, *antiparallel* if opposite. Everything else is loops. The program DSSP assigns secondary structure from 3D coordinates.
- **Tertiary**: the complete 3D **fold** of one chain, driven largely by hiding hydrophobic side chains from water.
- **Quaternary**: several chains assembled together. Hemoglobin is two α and two β chains.

Many proteins also contain **intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs)** with no stable shape, such as flexible linkers and signaling tails. p53's N-terminal region is disordered, while its central DNA-binding region is folded.

## Domains

A **domain** is a compact unit that folds on its own, often has a specific job, and recurs across many proteins. Proteins are often built from several domains like Lego. A receptor tyrosine kinase, for example, has a ligand-binding domain outside the cell, one **transmembrane** helix crossing the membrane, and a kinase domain inside. **Pfam** describes each domain family with a **profile HMM** (hidden Markov model), a statistical model of which residues are likely at each position, built from an alignment of family members. PF00069 = protein kinase domain. **InterPro** merges Pfam with other domain databases (SMART, PROSITE, CATH-Gene3D) into entries like IPR000719. **CATH** and **SCOP** classify domains by fold and are used to build train/test splits that separate folds (see Unit 7). Common domains: **SH2** (binds phosphorylated tyrosine), **SH3** (binds proline-rich segments), zinc finger and homeobox (both bind DNA), and the immunoglobulin (Ig) fold.

## AlphaFold confidence: pLDDT and PAE

AlphaFold predicts 3D coordinates for every residue and also says how much to trust them.

- **pLDDT** (predicted local distance difference test, 0–100): per-residue confidence, stored in the "B-factor" column of AlphaFold's PDB files (a column that normally records atomic mobility in experimental structures). **>90** very high, side chains usually right. **70–90** confident, backbone good. **50–70** low. **<50** very low, often a disordered region. Low-pLDDT regions look like floating ribbons and should not be read as real structure.
- **PAE** (predicted aligned error): a residue × residue matrix. Entry (x, y) is the expected error, in **Å** (ångströms, 10⁻¹⁰ m), in residue x's position if you superimpose the prediction on the true structure using residue y. Low-error blocks mark confidently placed domains. High PAE between two domains means their **relative orientation** is uncertain, even when each domain alone has high pLDDT.
- For complexes (several chains): **pTM** and **ipTM** (interface predicted TM-score). ipTM above ~0.8 suggests a confident interface; below ~0.6 suggests a likely failed one.
- Comparing two structures: **RMSD** (root-mean-square deviation of atom positions after superposition, in Å; sensitive to outliers), **TM-score** (0–1; above 0.5 generally means the same fold), **GDT_TS** (CASP's classic score), and **lDDT** (compares local inter-atom distances, so no superposition is needed).

## MSAs and coevolution

**Homologs** are proteins descended from a common ancestor; they usually share structure. A **multiple sequence alignment (MSA)** lines up many homologs so that each column holds the equivalent position in every sequence. Homologs are found by search tools such as JackHMMER, HHblits or **MMseqs2** (which ColabFold uses). An MSA carries two signals:

1. **Conservation**: a column that never changes across evolution is probably essential, so mutations there are likely harmful. Variant-effect tools such as SIFT and EVE build on this.
2. **Coevolution**: if residues i and j touch in 3D, a mutation at one is often compensated by a change at the other, so their columns vary together. Statistical models (Potts models, **direct coupling analysis**, or DCA) extract these pairs to predict which residues are in contact. AlphaFold2's Evoformer learns this from the MSA. Protein language models such as **ESM-2** absorb similar information from millions of single sequences, which is why ESMFold works without an MSA.

The depth of an MSA is often summarized as **Neff**, the effective number of sequences after down-weighting near-duplicates. Low Neff (few homologs, as in "orphan" or designed proteins) means less signal and worse predictions.

## Families worth knowing

- **Protein kinases** (~500 in humans, the "kinome"): **enzymes** that move a phosphate group from **ATP** (the cell's energy molecule) onto Ser/Thr or Tyr of a target protein. They share a two-lobed fold with the ATP pocket between the lobes, plus conserved features (the activation loop, the DFG motif, a "gatekeeper" residue). Most kinase drugs (imatinib, osimertinib) are **inhibitors** that compete with ATP in that pocket.
- **GPCRs** (G protein-coupled receptors; ~800 human genes): **receptors** with seven transmembrane helices. They detect hormones, neurotransmitters, odors and light, and signal through G proteins and arrestins. Roughly a third of approved drugs target them. Pfam 7tm_1 covers the largest class, rhodopsin-like class A.
- **Ion channels**: membrane proteins forming pores that let specific ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) through. They open in response to voltage or a bound ligand.
- **Transcription factors** (~1,600 human): bind specific DNA motifs to regulate genes (see Unit 1), through DNA-binding domains: C2H2 zinc fingers (the largest class), homeodomains, bHLH, bZIP, nuclear receptors, forkhead.
- **Antibodies (IgG)**: immune proteins that bind a target molecule, the **antigen**. Y-shaped, built from two heavy and two light chains joined by disulfide bonds. The two **Fab** arms bind antigen and the **Fc** stem recruits immune cells. Each arm's variable domains (VH, VL) carry three loops called **complementarity-determining regions (CDRs)**. **CDR-H3** (heavy chain, loop 3) is the most diverse and usually dominates binding. Diversity comes from **V(D)J recombination**, where gene segments are shuffled in each B cell, plus somatic hypermutation. Camelid **nanobodies (VHH)** are single-domain antibodies. Antibody language models train on the OAS repertoire database.
- Others: proteases (cut proteins), E3 ubiquitin ligases (see Unit 1), and enzymes classified by **EC numbers** (EC 2.7.11.1 = a Ser/Thr kinase class).

## How you'll see this in a paper

- "Residues with pLDDT < 70 were masked": discarding low-confidence or disordered regions.
- "The predicted complex had ipTM 0.85": a confident interface prediction.
- "MSAs generated with ColabFold's MMseqs2 pipeline against UniRef30."
- "ESM-2 650M embeddings, mean-pooled over residues": one vector per protein from a protein language model.
- "Mutations cluster in the activation loop / DNA-binding domain": interpreting variants by domain.
