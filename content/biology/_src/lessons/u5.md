### Key terms

- **Signaling pathway**: a chain of molecules passing a message from a signal to a response inside the cell.
- **Ligand**: a signal molecule that binds a receptor.
- **Receptor**: a protein that binds a signal molecule and relays its message.
- **Growth factor**: a protein signal telling cells to grow and divide.
- **Cytokine**: a protein signal coordinating immune responses.
- **Kinase**: an enzyme that adds a phosphate group to a protein.
- **Phosphatase**: an enzyme that removes a phosphate group from a protein.
- **Phosphorylation**: the addition of a phosphate group (written p-ERK or pS473).
- **GTPase**: a protein switch that is ON when bound to GTP and OFF after cutting it to GDP.
- **GEF**: a protein that switches a small GTPase ON by swapping its GDP for GTP.
- **GAP**: a protein that switches a small GTPase OFF by speeding up its GTP hydrolysis.
- **Adaptor**: a protein without enzyme activity that links other proteins together.
- **Dimerization**: two proteins pairing up.
- **Transcription factor (TF)**: a protein that binds DNA to turn genes on or off (see Unit 1).
- **Target gene**: a gene whose transcription a given TF controls.
- **E3 ubiquitin ligase**: an enzyme that attaches the small "destroy me" tag protein to specific targets.
- **Proteasome**: the cellular machine that shreds tagged proteins.
- **Negative feedback**: a pathway's output switching off its own input.
- **Proliferation**: cells dividing and multiplying in number.
- **Oncogene**: a gene whose overactivity (gain of function) drives cancer.
- **Tumor suppressor**: a gene whose loss of function drives cancer.
- **Amplification**: extra copies of a gene in the genome.
- **Fusion gene**: two genes joined into one by a chromosome rearrangement.
- **Apoptosis**: programmed, orderly cell suicide.
- **Caspase**: a protein-cutting enzyme that executes programmed cell death.
- **Mitochondria**: the cell's energy-producing compartments, also central to programmed cell death.
- **Cell cycle**: the sequence of phases by which a cell copies its DNA and divides.
- **Mitosis**: the division step in which duplicated chromosomes separate.
- **Checkpoint**: a control point that halts division if something is wrong.
- **Cyclin**: a regulatory protein whose level rises and falls through division phases, activating a partner kinase.
- **CDK**: the kinase that is switched on by binding a cyclin.
- **Synthetic lethality**: losing either of two genes is survivable, but losing both kills the cell.
- **Western blot**: a lab assay that detects a specific protein (or phospho-form) with antibodies after gel separation.

## How to think about signaling

A **signaling pathway** carries information: a **ligand** (a growth factor, a hormone, or an immune **cytokine**) binds a **receptor**, or an internal sensor detects damage. The message then passes along a chain of molecular switches. The two main switches are:

1. **Phosphorylation**: a **kinase** attaches a phosphate group to a protein, changing its shape and activity. A **phosphatase** removes it.
2. **GTP binding**: small **GTPase** proteins are ON when holding the molecule GTP and OFF after cutting it to GDP. A **GEF** turns them on by swapping GDP for GTP; a **GAP** turns them off by speeding up GTP cutting.

The chain ends at **transcription factors**, which change which genes are expressed, or at other effectors that change growth, metabolism, division or survival. **Negative feedback**, where the output dampens its own input, is everywhere.

Cancer is largely broken signaling. **Oncogenes** are stuck accelerators: a **gain-of-function** change (an activating mutation, extra gene copies from **amplification**, or a **fusion** of two genes) and one altered copy is enough. **Tumor suppressors** are broken brakes: **loss of function**, and usually both copies must be lost (Knudson's **two-hit** hypothesis).

## RTK → RAS → MAPK (proliferation)

1. A growth factor such as EGF binds a **receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)**, a membrane receptor whose inner part is a kinase that phosphorylates tyrosines. Examples: EGFR/ERBB family, MET, ALK. Two receptors **dimerize** (pair up) and phosphorylate each other (**autophosphorylation**).
2. The **adaptor** protein **GRB2** grabs those phosphotyrosines with its SH2 domain (see Unit 4) and brings **SOS**, a GEF, which turns on **RAS** (KRAS, NRAS, HRAS) by loading GTP.
3. RAS-GTP activates the kinase **RAF** (ARAF, BRAF, CRAF), which phosphorylates **MEK1/2**, which phosphorylate **ERK1/2** (official symbols MAPK3/MAPK1). This kinase chain is the **MAPK cascade** (mitogen-activated protein kinase; mitogen = something that triggers division). ERK enters the nucleus and switches on growth genes (via FOS, MYC).
4. RAS switches itself off by cutting GTP, helped by **GAPs** such as **NF1**.

In cancer: **KRAS G12/G13/Q61** mutations block GTP cutting, so RAS stays on (common in pancreatic, colorectal and lung cancer). **BRAF V600E** makes BRAF permanently active (about half of melanomas). **EGFR** L858R and exon 19 deletions occur in lung adenocarcinoma. Losing NF1 removes a brake. Drugs: EGFR inhibitors (osimertinib), BRAF inhibitors (vemurafenib, dabrafenib), MEK inhibitors (trametinib), KRAS G12C inhibitors (sotorasib).

## PI3K → AKT → mTOR (growth and survival)

RTKs also activate **PI3K**, a lipid kinase that phosphorylates the membrane lipid PIP2 into **PIP3**. Its catalytic subunit p110α is encoded by **PIK3CA**. PIP3 acts as a docking flag that recruits the kinase **AKT** to the membrane, where it is activated by phosphorylation at **T308 (by PDK1)** and **S473 (by mTORC2)**. AKT promotes survival and growth. It inhibits the **TSC1/TSC2** complex, a GAP for the GTPase **RHEB**; with that brake off, RHEB activates **mTORC1**, a kinase complex that senses nutrients and boosts protein production by phosphorylating **S6K** and **4E-BP1**. The energy sensor AMPK inhibits mTORC1 when fuel is low. **PTEN** is a phosphatase that converts PIP3 back to PIP2, which makes it a major tumor suppressor. The drug **rapamycin** inhibits mTORC1. PIK3CA cancer hotspots: E545K, H1047R.

## p53 and the DNA damage response

DNA damage must be detected before the cell copies or divides its DNA.
- Sensor kinases: **ATM** responds to double-strand breaks (both strands cut); **ATR** responds to single-stranded DNA and stalled DNA copying ("replication stress"). They activate the checkpoint kinases **CHK2** and **CHK1** respectively, and both lines phosphorylate **p53** (e.g., Ser15).
- Normally p53 is kept scarce by **MDM2**, an **E3 ubiquitin ligase** that tags p53 for destruction by the proteasome. MDM2 is itself a p53 **target gene**, so p53 induces its own destroyer, a negative feedback loop. Damage-triggered phosphorylation blocks MDM2 binding, so p53 builds up.
- p53 is a **transcription factor**. Its targets include **CDKN1A (p21)**, which pauses the cell cycle; **MDM2**; and the cell-death genes **BAX**, **PUMA (BBC3)** and **NOXA (PMAIP1)**. Outcomes: pause and repair, permanent arrest (**senescence**), or **apoptosis**.
- **TP53** is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer. Most mutations are missense changes in the DNA-binding domain (hotspots R175, R248, R273). Inherited TP53 mutations cause **Li-Fraumeni syndrome**, a high-risk cancer syndrome. **BRCA1/BRCA2** repair broken DNA by **homologous recombination** (using the sister copy as a template). Tumors lacking them are killed by **PARP inhibitors**, an example of **synthetic lethality**: losing either repair route is survivable, but losing both is not.

## Wnt / β-catenin (development, stem cells)

Without a Wnt signal, a **destruction complex** (APC, AXIN, GSK3β, CK1) phosphorylates **β-catenin** (gene CTNNB1), which then gets ubiquitinated and destroyed. When Wnt binds its receptor **Frizzled** and co-receptor **LRP5/6**, the complex is blocked, β-catenin accumulates, enters the nucleus, and partners with **TCF/LEF** transcription factors to switch on targets (MYC, AXIN2, LGR5). Loss of **APC** starts most colorectal cancers.

## NF-κB (inflammation)

The transcription factor **NF-κB** (classically the pair RELA/p65 + p50) is held in the cytoplasm by an inhibitor, **IκB**. Inflammatory signals (the cytokines TNF and IL-1, or **Toll-like receptors** that detect microbes) activate the **IKK complex** (IKKα, IKKβ, NEMO). IKK phosphorylates IκBα, marking it for destruction, so NF-κB is freed to enter the nucleus and turn on inflammatory and survival genes.

## JAK-STAT (cytokine signaling)

Many cytokine receptors (for interferons, interleukins, EPO) have no kinase of their own. Instead they carry **JAK** kinases (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2). Ligand binding activates the JAKs, which phosphorylate the receptor and the **STAT** proteins. Phosphorylated STATs dimerize and enter the nucleus as transcription factors. **SOCS** proteins provide negative feedback. **JAK2 V617F** drives myeloproliferative neoplasms (blood cancers with overproduction of blood cells). JAK inhibitor: ruxolitinib.

## Apoptosis (programmed cell death)

**Apoptosis** is orderly cell suicide, carried out by **caspases**, protein-cutting enzymes (cysteine proteases that cut after aspartate).
- **Intrinsic (mitochondrial) route**: stress shifts the balance within the **BCL-2 family**. "BH3-only" proteins (BIM, BID, PUMA, NOXA) overpower the protective members (BCL-2, BCL-XL, MCL-1), letting **BAX/BAK** punch holes in the outer membrane of the **mitochondria** (MOMP, mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization). **Cytochrome c** leaks out and assembles with **APAF1** into the **apoptosome**, which activates **caspase-9**, then the executioner **caspases-3/7**. The drug **venetoclax** blocks BCL-2.
- **Extrinsic route**: "death ligands" (FASL, TNF, TRAIL) bind death receptors, which recruit **FADD** and activate **caspase-8**. Caspase-8 also cuts BID, linking to the intrinsic route.
- Lab markers: cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP.

## The cell cycle

A dividing cell goes **G1** (growth) → **S** (DNA synthesis: the genome is copied) → **G2** (preparation) → **M** (**mitosis**: chromosomes separate and the cell splits). Non-dividing cells rest in **G0**. The cycle is driven by **cyclins**, proteins whose levels rise and fall with the cycle, each switching on a partner **cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)**:
- **Cyclin D–CDK4/6** (G1) phosphorylates the brake protein **RB**, releasing **E2F** transcription factors that switch on S-phase genes. Passing the **restriction point** commits the cell to dividing.
- **Cyclin E–CDK2** (G1/S), **cyclin A–CDK2** (S), **cyclin B–CDK1** (G2/M).
- CDK inhibitors: the **INK4** family (p16^INK4a^, from CDKN2A) blocks CDK4/6; the **CIP/KIP** family (p21, p27) blocks CDK2/CDK1.
- **Checkpoints** halt the cycle when something is wrong: **G1/S** (DNA damage → p53 → p21), **G2/M** (damage → CHK1/2 → CDK1 kept off), and the **spindle assembly checkpoint**, which blocks chromosome separation (anaphase) until every chromosome is attached to the spindle (MAD2 inhibits APC/C–CDC20).
- CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib, abemaciclib) treat hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer.

## Oncogenes and tumor suppressors to know

**Oncogenes**: KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, EGFR, ERBB2 (HER2, amplified in some breast cancers), PIK3CA, MYC (amplified), ABL1 (the BCR-ABL fusion in chronic myeloid leukemia, treated with imatinib), ALK fusions, CCND1, MDM2 (amplified). **Tumor suppressors**: TP53, RB1, PTEN, APC, CDKN2A, BRCA1/2, NF1, VHL, SMAD4, ARID1A.

## How you'll see this in a paper

- "KRAS-mutant vs wild-type", "BRAF^V600E^ melanoma": samples grouped by driver mutation (wild-type = not mutated).
- "Hallmark gene sets (MSigDB): E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, TNFA signaling via NFKB, P53 pathway": pathway activity scores computed from RNA-seq.
- "pERK/ERK ratio": a readout of MAPK activity.
- Perturbation-prediction models are often judged on whether they recover known pathway structure.
