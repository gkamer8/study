### Key terms

- **Nucleotide**: one building block of DNA or RNA: a sugar, a phosphate group, and a base (A, C, G, T; U replaces T in RNA).
- **Base pair (bp)**: two bases on opposite DNA strands held together (A with T, G with C); also the unit of DNA length (1 kb = 1,000 of them).
- **Strand**: one continuous chain of nucleotides; DNA has two wound into a double helix.
- **5' end**: the end of a nucleic-acid chain carrying a free phosphate; sequences are written starting from it ("upstream").
- **3' end**: the end of a nucleic-acid chain carrying a free hydroxyl group, where polymerases add new nucleotides ("downstream").
- **Antiparallel**: describes the two DNA strands running in opposite directions.
- **Reverse complement**: the sequence of the opposite strand, read in its own 5'→3' direction (complement every base, then reverse the order).
- **Chromosome**: one long DNA molecule plus its packaging proteins; humans have 23 kinds.
- **Genome**: all the DNA of an organism.
- **Haploid**: having one copy of each chromosome (n), like eggs and sperm.
- **Diploid**: having two copies of each chromosome (2n), like most human cells.
- **Autosome**: any of the numbered human chromosomes 1–22 (not X or Y).
- **Sex chromosome**: X or Y.
- **Gene**: a stretch of DNA that is copied into a functional RNA (and often then into a protein).
- **Locus**: a specific position or region in the genome.
- **Allele**: one version of the sequence at a given position in the genome.
- **Homozygous**: carrying two identical versions of the sequence at a position.
- **Heterozygous**: carrying two different versions of the sequence at a position.
- **Transcription**: copying DNA into RNA.
- **Translation**: building a protein by reading an mRNA template.
- **mRNA**: the processed RNA copy of a gene that ribosomes read to make protein ("messenger").
- **Ribosome**: the molecular machine that reads mRNA and builds protein.
- **Amino acid**: one of the 20 building blocks of proteins.
- **Codon**: three consecutive nucleotides that specify one amino acid or a stop.
- **Exon**: a segment of a gene that is kept in the mature mRNA.
- **Intron**: a segment of a gene that is cut out of the RNA before it is used.
- **Splicing**: removing introns from RNA and joining the remaining segments.
- **UTR**: a stretch at either end of an mRNA that is not turned into protein.
- **CDS**: the part of an mRNA that is translated, from start codon to stop codon.
- **Promoter**: DNA at the start of a gene where RNA polymerase assembles.
- **Enhancer**: a regulatory DNA element that boosts a gene's transcription, often from far away.
- **Transcription factor (TF)**: a protein that binds a specific short DNA motif to turn genes up or down.
- **Transcript**: any single RNA molecule produced from a gene.
- **Isoform**: one of several alternative RNA (or protein) versions made from the same gene.
- **Reading frame**: the way a sequence is chopped into consecutive codons.
- **Variant**: any difference from the reference sequence.
- **SNP**: a single-base difference that is common in the population.
- **PTM**: a chemical change made to a protein after it has been built.

## DNA as a data structure

DNA is a polymer, meaning a long chain of repeating units. Each unit is a **nucleotide**: a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate group, and one of four **bases**: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), thymine (T). For data purposes, DNA is a string over the alphabet {A, C, G, T}.

DNA is **double-stranded**: two chains wind into a helix, held together by **base pairing**. A always pairs with T, and G always pairs with C. So each strand fully determines the other ("complementary"). Lengths are given in **base pairs (bp)**: 1 kb = 1,000 bp, 1 Mb = 10⁶ bp, 1 Gb = 10⁹ bp.

### What 5' and 3' mean, and why direction matters

The carbons in the deoxyribose sugar are numbered 1' to 5' ("one-prime" to "five-prime"; the prime distinguishes sugar carbons from atoms in the base). In a strand, the phosphate attached to one sugar's **5' carbon** links to the **3' carbon** of the previous sugar. So the backbone has a built-in direction: one end of the strand has a free 5' phosphate (the **5' end**), and the other has a free 3' hydroxyl (the **3' end**).

Direction matters because the enzymes that copy DNA and make RNA (**polymerases**) can only add new nucleotides to the 3' end. So all copying runs **5'→3'**, and ribosomes also read mRNA 5'→3'. By convention, **every sequence is written 5'→3', left to right**. "Upstream" means toward the 5' end of a gene and "downstream" means toward the 3' end.

The two strands are **antiparallel**: they run in opposite directions. If one strand is 5'-ATGC-3', its partner is 3'-TACG-5', which written in the standard direction is 5'-GCAT-3'. That operation, complement every base then reverse, is the **reverse complement**, and you will compute it constantly.

### Chromosomes, haploid and diploid

DNA is packaged into **chromosomes**, each one long DNA molecule wrapped around proteins called **histones** (the DNA-protein complex is **chromatin**). Humans have 22 numbered **autosomes** plus the sex chromosomes X and Y.

Most human cells carry **two copies** of each chromosome, one from each parent. This is **diploid** (2n). Eggs and sperm carry **one copy** of each and are **haploid** (n). When we say the human genome is "3.1 billion base pairs (haploid)", we mean one copy of each chromosome adds up to ~3.1 Gb. A typical cell holds about twice that. There is also a small separate **mitochondrial genome** (~16.6 kb, circular, called chrM; mitochondria are the cell's energy-producing compartments), inherited from the mother.

### Genes, loci and alleles

A **gene** is a region of DNA that gets copied into RNA that does something. Usually that RNA is translated into a protein, but some genes make RNA that functions on its own. A **locus** is just a position or region ("the TP53 locus"). Because you have two copies of each autosome, you have two **alleles** (versions) at each locus. If they match you are **homozygous**; if they differ, **heterozygous**. A difference from the reference sequence is a **variant**. A single-base difference is a **SNV**, or a **SNP** when it is common in the population.

A **reference genome** stores one haploid sequence, and only the **forward (plus, +) strand**. A gene can lie on either strand. TP53, for example, sits on the **minus strand** of chromosome 17, so its RNA matches the reverse complement of the reference there.

Positions are written as coordinates (chr17:7,668,402) or as **cytogenetic bands**, which are named after the stripes chromosomes show under a microscope. Each chromosome has a constriction (the **centromere**) splitting it into a short arm (**p**) and a long arm (**q**). So 17p13.1 = chromosome 17, short arm, region 1, band 3, sub-band 1.

Only ~**1.5%** of the genome codes for protein. There are ~**20,000 protein-coding genes** and a larger number of non-coding genes, ~60,000 genes in all. About half the genome derives from repetitive "jumping" DNA (**transposons**, e.g. Alu and LINE-1 elements).

## The central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein

**Transcription**: the enzyme **RNA polymerase II** copies a gene's DNA into **RNA**. RNA is single-stranded, uses the sugar ribose, and has **uracil (U)** in place of T. The RNA matches the gene's **coding (sense) strand**; the polymerase reads the opposite **template** strand.

**Translation**: a **ribosome** reads a **messenger RNA (mRNA)** three letters at a time and joins the matching **amino acids** into a protein chain. Proteins do most of the work in cells: they act as enzymes, structure, signals, and receptors.

## Anatomy of a protein-coding gene

Reading 5'→3' along the gene's own strand:

1. **Promoter**: DNA just upstream of the **transcription start site (TSS)**, the first base copied into RNA. RNA polymerase and helper proteins assemble here. Many human promoters sit in **CpG islands**, stretches rich in the dinucleotide C-then-G ("p" = the phosphate between them). Methylation of these Cs (adding a methyl group) generally silences a promoter.
2. **Exons and introns**: the whole gene is transcribed into a **pre-mRNA**. **Introns** are cut out by **splicing**, and **exons** are stitched together. Human genes average ~8–10 exons, and introns are often far longer than exons.
3. **5' UTR** (untranslated region): exon sequence before the start codon. Transcribed but not translated.
4. **Coding sequence (CDS)**: from the **start codon** (ATG, encoding methionine) to a **stop codon** (TAA, TAG or TGA). It usually spans several exons.
5. **3' UTR**: after the stop codon. It contains binding sites for **microRNAs** and the **polyadenylation signal** (AATAAA), after which the RNA is cut and a **poly(A) tail** (a string of As) is added.

The mature mRNA also gets a protective **5' cap**. Note that exon ≠ coding: the first and last exons usually contain UTR.

**Enhancers** are regulatory DNA elements that boost a gene's transcription from a distance, often tens to hundreds of kilobases away, upstream, downstream, or inside introns. DNA folds into loops that bring enhancers next to promoters, mostly within neighborhoods called **TADs** (topologically associating domains) whose borders are set by the protein CTCF together with cohesin. **Transcription factors (TFs)** are proteins that recognize short DNA "words" (**motifs**, ~6–12 bp) in promoters and enhancers. Most disease-associated variants found by **GWAS** (genome-wide association studies, which scan many people for variants correlated with a trait) fall outside genes, often in enhancers. That's a big reason genomics ML models (Enformer, Borzoi) predict regulatory activity from long DNA windows.

## Transcripts, isoforms, splicing

One gene typically yields several **transcripts** (RNA products). Alternative versions are called **isoforms**. They arise from different promoters, **alternative splicing** (skipping an exon, using a different splice site, keeping an intron, choosing between two exons), and different poly(A) sites. Databases pick a representative transcript: **MANE Select** is the one transcript per protein-coding gene that the two big annotation groups (NCBI RefSeq and Ensembl; see Unit 3) agreed to match exactly. It is the default for clinical variant reporting.

Introns almost always begin with **GT** and end with **AG** (GU…AG in RNA). A molecular machine of RNA and protein, the **spliceosome**, recognizes these sites. Variants at the first two or last two bases of an intron ("canonical splice sites") usually break splicing. SpliceAI is the standard ML model for predicting such effects.

## Codons and reading frames

The ribosome reads mRNA in non-overlapping triplets called **codons**. 4³ = 64 codons: 61 encode the 20 amino acids and 3 mean "stop". The code is **degenerate** (redundant): most amino acids have several codons, and the third base often doesn't matter (the "wobble" position). Variant types follow:

- **Synonymous** (silent): the codon changes but the amino acid doesn't.
- **Missense**: a different amino acid.
- **Nonsense**: creates a premature stop codon. mRNAs with an early stop well before the last exon are usually destroyed by **nonsense-mediated decay (NMD)**, a cellular quality-control system.
- **Frameshift**: an insertion or deletion (**indel**) whose length isn't a multiple of 3 shifts how every later codon is read, garbling the rest of the protein.
- **In-frame indel**: a multiple of 3, which adds or removes whole amino acids.

A **reading frame** is one way of chopping a sequence into codons. There are three per strand, so **six** in total. An **open reading frame (ORF)** runs from a start codon to a stop with no stop in between.

## After translation

Proteins headed outside the cell or into membranes begin with a **signal peptide**, a short sequence at the protein's start (its **N-terminus**) that is later cut off. **Post-translational modifications (PTMs)** then tune proteins:

- **Phosphorylation**: adding a phosphate to serine (S), threonine (T) or tyrosine (Y). It is done by enzymes called **kinases** and undone by **phosphatases**, and it is the main on/off switch in cell signaling.
- **Ubiquitination**: attaching the small protein ubiquitin to lysine (K), done by **E3 ligases**. Chains linked through ubiquitin's K48 mark a protein for destruction by the **proteasome** (the cell's protein shredder).
- **Acetylation / methylation** of lysine (and arginine), notably on histones (e.g. H3K27ac = histone H3, lysine 27, acetylated).
- **Glycosylation**: adding sugars, on N in the motif N-X-S/T (X ≠ P) or on S/T.
- **Disulfide bonds**: covalent links between two cysteines (C).

## Not everything is protein-coding

- **rRNA, tRNA**: parts of the translation machinery.
- **miRNA**: ~22-nucleotide RNAs that repress target mRNAs.
- **lncRNA**: long (>200 nt) non-coding RNAs (e.g. XIST).
- **snRNA**: parts of the spliceosome.
- **Pseudogenes**: broken copies of genes. They can attract reads that belong to the real gene.

## How you'll see this in a paper

- "We used the MANE Select transcript": one isoform per gene defines the coordinates and protein sequence.
- "Variants annotated as missense, synonymous, stop-gained, splice-donor": the consequence classes above.
- "196 kb of sequence centered on the TSS" (Enformer): long context, because enhancers are distal.
- Protein language models train on already-spliced, translated protein sequences. DNA models see introns, UTRs and intergenic sequence.
